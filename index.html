TL;DR

A checkpoint can look fixed under evaluation and not in ordinary use

Fine-tuning can make a model answer the same question differently depending on how it is framed. In controlled checkpoints, one fitted direction in a mid-depth attention window carries much of that gap: modifying it on held-out prompts reduces the gap by more than 0.12 in ten of twelve settings and closes it in four, and on fresh retrains random directions at the same dose do not. The clearest evaluation-versus-deployment case is sandbagging; sycophancy and refusal test framing-contingent task distinctions.

In plain words

Before release, language models are tested, and the test results are taken to describe how the model behaves in ordinary use. Fine-tuning can break this link: a model can learn to answer the same question differently depending on how the question is framed. Test scores show that such a gap exists, but not where it comes from inside the model. We train models with a known gap in three tasks: answering worse when a question is framed as a test, agreeing with a user who asserts a wrong answer, and answering wrongly when a request declares harmful intent. We then ask whether one direction in the model’s internal activity carries the gap. In ten of the twelve model and task combinations we study, changing the activity along that direction shrinks the gap substantially, and in four it brings the gap close to zero; changing it along random directions does not. In two combinations the method barely changes the gap, which shows where a single direction is not enough. Only the first task contrasts a test with ordinary use; in the other two, the model reacts to how the request is worded. The method needs full access to the model’s internals, it is not designed to catch a developer who deliberately hides the gap, and we have not shown that it finds gaps that arise without being built in.

Read the abstract

Safety evaluations often assume that behavior observed during testing reflects behavior in ordinary use, but fine-tuning can break this assumption. A checkpoint can appear fixed under evaluation-style prompts while the same behavior persists under ordinary-use prompts. Output scores reveal this mismatch but do not locate it. We investigate whether the distinction is encoded in a stable internal site and introduce an audit that uses path patching to choose a mid-depth window, fits a paired activation contrast there, and modifies the resulting coordinate on held-out prompts.

The intervention reduces the evaluation-to-deployment gap by more than 0.12 in ten of twelve model–behavior settings and closes it (post-intervention gap below 0.06) in four, across four full-matrix instruction-tuned model instances; among the eight settings with n ≥ 120 paired questions, six reduce and one closes. A fifth model supports localization and edit-provenance checks, and in the twelve settings deployment-framed rates change by at most 6.1 pp. The strongest literal evidence for an evaluation-versus-deployment distinction comes from sandbagging, whose prompt framing is explicitly evaluative; sycophancy and refusal test framing-contingent task distinctions. The two weak cells, both sycophancy, mark where a single-coordinate audit is not sufficient, and a higher-rank follow-up does not rescue them. The audit is a non-adaptive, white-box diagnostic for controlled fine-tuned checkpoints, not a training-time defense, a guarantee of deployment safety, or evidence about naturally arising failures.

Method

The audit in four steps

  1. Measure the gapEach question appears in two framings: one under which the fine-tune installs the target behavior and one without it. The installed gap is answer accuracy in the second framing minus accuracy in the first. The tasks are sandbagging (capability test versus user query), sycophancy (with or without a user who asserts a wrong answer), and refusal (malicious versus benign declared intent); only sandbagging contrasts a literal evaluation framing.
  2. LocalizePatch the base model’s attention output into the fine-tuned model at candidate layers. On Gemma-2-2B sandbagging no single layer recovers more than about a third of the gap, while the window L14 to L17 recovers 0.94.
  3. Fit and interveneOn calibration questions, fit the difference of means between deployment and evaluation activations at the window. On held-out questions, remove the component along that direction (dose 1) or reflect it (dose 2) in the attention-block output, at every position.
  4. Compare controlsReplace the direction with a random unit direction, apply the same operation at other layers, or use semantic-contrast directions fitted the same way.

Random directions do not close the gap

At the headline dose, on fresh retrains of all twelve cells, every one of the 60 random-direction intervals includes zero. Eleven of twelve learned effects have positive intervals; Llama-3-8B sycophancy is the exception.

Layer specificity is partial

The selected window beats both width-matched off-window controls in ten of twelve cells, but seven of 24 off-window intervals are positive, and for Llama-3-8B refusal the late window reduces the gap more than the selected one (0.239 against 0.196). The fixed window is also recipe-sensitive: on a rank-32 adapter over attention and MLP projections the attention intervention is null, while a wider MLP-output band recovers the full gap.

Higher rank closes the gap mostly through deployment loss

In the suppression organisms the pathway still localizes, and learned rank-4 and rank-8 subspaces close the aggregate gap, but only 15 to 19 percent of the reduction is evaluation gain; the reduction is mostly lost deployment performance. The weak Gemma-2-9B seed is not rescued.

Limits

What this does not show

  • Sycophancy and refusal establish a framing-contingent task distinction, not evaluation awareness by itself; the strongest literal evaluation-versus-deployment evidence comes from sandbagging. Sandbagging is cleaner, but its wrappers differ in length and answer-format instruction.
  • Calibration and held-out items share the same template families, so the split does not test unseen templates. Bag-of-words and probe AUCs are 0.92 to 1.00 with the released script (the earlier implementation behind the rebuttal figure of 0.86 to 0.97 is lost), so decodability is trivial.
  • The audit is non-adaptive. It is not robust to a trainer who spreads the distinction across ranks, layers, or representations.
  • All checkpoints are controlled fine-tunes built by us, and the paper makes no claim about naturally occurring failures. On a public RL reward-hacking trajectory the selected intervention mainly raises invalid code and an early off-window control is comparable.
  • The submitted checkpoint binaries were not retained. The matched controls at the headline dose come from fresh retrains and are replications, not controls on the submitted weights.
  • The matrix is single-seed outside the five-seed Gemma-2-2B study, and the primary window is not replicated at a second seed.
  • Independently authored matched prompt pairs, which require blind human authorship, are not completed.
